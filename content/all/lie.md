## Definition
For a Lie group $G$, its Lie algebra is first defined simply as the tangent space at the identity and to define the Lie bracket on $T_e G$, consider the left-invariant vector field $\widetilde X(g)=(dL_g)_eX$ then define $[X,Y]_{\mathfrak g} = [\widetilde X,\widetilde Y](e)$.

For a matrix Lie group, $\widetilde X(g) = (dL_g)_eX=gX$. With this convention, the Lie bracket becomes the usual matrix commutator $[X,Y]=XY-YX$. If we use the right-invariant vector field, the Lie bracket should be $[X^R,Y^R]=-[X,Y]^R$ where $X^R(g)=Xg$. Proof:
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\widetilde X,\widetilde Y]f(g)
&=\widetilde X(\widetilde Yf)(g) -\widetilde Y(\widetilde Xf)(g)\\
&=D(\widetilde Yf)_g[\widetilde X(g)] -\cdots\\
&=D(Df_g[\widetilde Y(g)])_g[\widetilde X(g)] - \cdots \\
&=D^2f_g[\widetilde Y(g), \widetilde X(g)] + Df_g[ D\widetilde Y_g[\widetilde X(g)]] - \cdots \\
&=D^2f_g[\widetilde Y(g), \widetilde X(g)] + Df_g[ D\widetilde Y_g[gX]] - \cdots \\
&=D^2f_g[\widetilde Y(g), \widetilde X(g)] + Df_g[\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\widetilde Y(g\exp(tX))] - \cdots \\
&=D^2f_g[\widetilde Y(g), \widetilde X(g)]+Df_g[gXY] - \cdots \\
&=Df_g[g(XY-YX)]
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $[X,Y]_{\mathfrak g} = [\widetilde X,\widetilde Y](e)=XY-YX$

## Application in differential equations

Consider an ODE, $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = f(x,y)$. It is a hypersurface
$G(x,y,p) := f(x,y) - p = 0$ in the first jet space $J^1$, where
$p = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$. Suppose the equation admits a one-parameter
group of point symmetries generated by $X = \xi\partial_x + \eta\partial_y$.
The corresponding infinitesimal transformations are
$x' = x + \varepsilon\xi(x,y) + O(\varepsilon^2)$ and
$y' = y + \varepsilon\eta(x,y) + O(\varepsilon^2)$. We hope a solution after
transformation is still a solution, which informally means
$XG\big|_{G=0} = 0$. But when applying $X$ to $G(x,y,p)$, we have to determine
the transform of $p$. Since

$$
p' = \frac{\mathrm{d}y'}{\mathrm{d}x'}
   = \frac{\mathrm{d}y'/\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}x'/\mathrm{d}x}
   = \frac{p + \varepsilon(\eta_x + \eta_y p)}{1 + \varepsilon(\xi_x + \xi_y p)}
   = p + \varepsilon\bigl(\eta_x + p(\eta_y - \xi_x) - p^2\xi_y\bigr)
     + O(\varepsilon^2),
$$

$X$ should be prolonged to
$X^{(1)} = \xi\partial_x + \eta\partial_y + \zeta\partial_p$, where
$\zeta = \eta_x + p(\eta_y - \xi_x) - p^2\xi_y$ (the first prolongation
formula). The condition $X^{(1)}G\big|_{G=0} = 0$ corresponds to

$$
\xi f_x + \eta f_y - \eta_x - f(\eta_y - \xi_x) + f^2\xi_y = 0.
$$

These are the requirements when we choose $\xi$ and $\eta$.

After obtaining a generator, the flow-box theorem guarantees the existence of
locally trivial coordinates $(R,S)$ satisfying $XR = 0$ and $XS = 1$. Rewrite
$G=0$ in the new coordinates as $\tilde{G}(R,S,T)=0$, where
$T = \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}R}$. Because
$T' = \mathrm{d}S'/\mathrm{d}R' = \mathrm{d}(S+\varepsilon)/\mathrm{d}R = T$,
we have $X^{(1)}(T) = 0$. Therefore,

$$
0 = X^{(1)}\tilde{G}\big|_{G=0}
  = (XR)\tilde{G}_R\big|_{G=0}
  + (XS)\tilde{G}_S\big|_{G=0}
  + \bigl(X^{(1)}T\bigr)\tilde{G}_T\big|_{G=0}
  = \tilde{G}_S\big|_{G=0}.
$$

So we can solve $T = H(R)$, that is,
$\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}R} = H(R)$.

Why can $X^{(1)}$ act on $T$? We only need to prove $T$ is a scalar field on
the jet space. In fact,

$$
T = \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}R}
  = \frac{S_x + pS_y}{R_x + pR_y}.
$$

How do we prove that the prolongation on $(x,y)$ is the same as on $(R,S)$?
A jet is an equivalence class of smooth curves defined by their local behavior
at a point, up to derivatives of some finite order. (It is like
$T_q^{\ast}M \cong \mathfrak{m}_q/\mathfrak{m}_q^2$.) A prolongation is the
action naturally induced by a transformation on these equivalence classes.
Thus, prolongation is a coordinate-independent geometric construction rather
than a differential formula tied to a particular choice of coordinates.
